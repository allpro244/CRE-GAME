# Simulation probe — 2026-10-10: growth, the fringe, and the league table

Continues `SIM_PROBE_2026-10-09.md` (rounds 1-7).

## Round 8 — why the outskirts stay empty

The owner's question, after the year-100 screenshots: "the city still has yet to fill the
outskirts." Population at year 100 averaged 58.6k from 22k over six Frontier towns: about 1% a
year, the national rate.

### What the town lacked

1. **A reason for employers to choose it.** Every trade grew at its national BLS trend, so every
   town grew like the average one. US states' employment growth differs from the nation's by
   persistent amounts. Blanchard & Katz (1992) put Arizona, Florida and Nevada about 3%/yr over it
   for 1950-90 and New York about 1% under, and young regions are the fast ones.
   - **Added (`regionalPull`, market.ts):** one persistent regional trend per town, drawn from the
     seed.
     - Its mean is set by how young the town is (the vacant-lot share; a Frontier town opens
       around +1.1%/yr). The spread across towns is 0.8%/yr.
     - It is braked by the town's unit cost against the nation's: pay (85%) and office/shed rent
       (15%), with a location pull of 4% of the log gap a year, a long-run elasticity of 0.4 over
       a decade (Bartik 1991).
     - Agglomeration adds 4% output per doubling of employment (Ciccone & Hall 1996; Combes et al.
       2008).
2. **A fringe that can pencil.** The flats rent spread ran 3.75x (floor 0.52, ceiling 1.95) across
   an island about a mile wide, against the 2-2.5x stated in `locationRentMult`'s own comment. In
   Alonso-Muth-Mills, rent differences price the commute, and the commute here is a short walk.
   The floor is now 0.80 (2.4x).
3. **Replacements held to the order book. This was a bug from round 5.** The teardown path took the
   best-clearing height whatever the book had ordered and netted it off afterwards. One 100-year
   run put 1.65M sf of flats in the pipeline in a window where the book had ordered 92k and owed
   none; flats vacancy hit 44%. A replacement's added space is now capped at what is owed for its
   use, one storey always allowed, as the start path already was.
4. **Builders who could actually take the job.** The street's pencil counted every firm's margin,
   including firms at their live-job limit or out of cash. Sites cleared on their numbers, no one
   could claim them, and the start unwound: up to 158 of 221 attempts in a decade on one seed. The
   pencil now counts only firms able to take a job this month.

### Measured: six 100-year Frontier towns (seeds 101, 202, 303, 404, 777, 4242), means

| | rounds 6-7 | + replacement cap | + cap + fringe floor |
|---|---|---|---|
| peak flats vacancy | 16.5% | 10.8% | 11.4% |
| peak office vacancy | 31.1% | 23.5% | 26.5% |
| floor space started | 8.6M | 4.9M | 4.9M |
| population at year 100 | 67.6k | 53.8k | 56.3k |

- **About 40% of all construction had been unordered replacements.** Holding them to the book
  removes most of the gluts.
- **The same buildings were housing growth.** Population falls with them.
- **What limits growth now is sites that pencil.** It is not orders: the flats book carries 50-100k
  sf owed for years, with vacancy near 3%.
- **Whether sites pencil depends on the rate era.** A town that opens on a 9% loan index (the early
  1980s) pencils almost nothing new until money cheapens, which is what US apartment building
  did then.
- **Retrying a failed feasibility study within the month does not help.** Measured: successful
  starts unchanged, attempts tripled. The site that fails once fails again.

### Still open

- **Local pay drifts to 1.3-1.5x national over a century.** Local tightness counts unfilled jobs,
  the nation's does not, and the nation runs slack. Most of the nominal gap is local prices (rent in
  the CPI), so housing is upstream of it too.
- **Rent level against replacement cost in a growing town.** In elastic growing cities rent sits at
  or above replacement cost at the margin (Glaeser, Gyourko & Saks 2005). Here the rent anchor to
  incomes may be holding it under.

## Round 9 — pay, prices and the shed market

### Local pay had a unit root

Pay grew at the national rate plus `tight` x 0.012 a month, so every month the town ran tighter
than the nation added to a gap nothing closed. Over two 100-year towns, local pay ended 1.25-1.36x
the nation's with local unemployment at the national level, a 9% real premium no labour market
explains. Employers read pay (`regionalPull`), so the town stopped being chosen from about year 40.

Regional pay is stationary relative to the nation (Blanchard & Katz 1992), and its level follows
local unemployment: the wage curve, with an elasticity of about -0.1 (Blanchflower & Oswald 1994).
The town's pay now closes, with a two-year half-life, toward

    national pay
    x local prices / national prices   (equal real pay; Rosen 1979, Roback 1982)
    x (local u / national u)^-0.10
    x (jobs / opening jobs)^0.04       (the agglomeration regionalPull credits)

Freezes and the owed raise still apply.

### The nation's rent was flat

The town's shelter inflation was read against national CPI, which says national real rent never
moves. Every point of rent a richer nation pays everywhere was booked as this town getting dear:
local prices ended 1.15-1.37x the nation's, above San Francisco, for a town of 50-70k. The share of
income spent on rent is about constant across US metros and over time (Davis & Ortalo-Magné 2011),
and HUD's median two-bed rent rose 2.6x over 2000-2025 against prices 1.86x. National shelter now
moves with national pay, in the CPI and in the league table.

After both changes, local prices run about 1.0-1.1x the nation's and local pay tracks them plus the
agglomeration premium.

### Sheds at the fringe

`locationRentMult` says a shed on the fringe "should read ~1.0" and calls industrial the narrowest
spread of the four classes. The floor of 0.62 made it 2.5x, wider than flats.

On free land a fringe shed yielded 2.6% on cost against 8.7% required, and the industrial site
pencil read 0.00 for most of a century. Industrial stock went from 342k to 352k sf while the town
tripled, and with supply unable to answer, rent did all the clearing: -65% / +183% over five-year
windows, the most volatile class in town. In real markets industrial is the steadiest.

The floor is now 0.95 (a 1.6x spread). Over two towns, industrial stock grows 41-54% over the
century instead of not at all.

Rent swings remain large (five-year windows of -55% to +110%). A 350-500k sf market is 10-15
warehouses, so one delivery moves vacancy 10-15 points. That is the real lumpiness of a small
market, left open.

## Round 10 — the island is the region's downtown

The island is about 1 km² (0.69 km² of lots) and holds roughly 1,300 homes, while population is
derived from jobs. So about 85% of the people the town counts must live on the mainland and commute
in. The Economy page now shows that split: region population, living on the island, and commuting
in, at 900 sf per home and 2.51 people per home (Census ACS household size).

- Flats demand moves one-for-one with households (elasticity 1.0).
- The in-migration choke throttled the WHOLE region whenever island flats were tight. That cut mean
  population from 63.6k to 54.9k on six seeds. It now weighs the island's share of homes, so the
  mainland grows on its own regional trend: 65.7k, peak unemployment 9.4%, peak flats vacancy 11.3%.

## Round 11 — lots grow outward

Platted residential lots scale with distance along the growth ring: a smoothstep from 30% out, up to
1.8x width and depth at the edge, and outer villa lots keep setbacks. The median lot runs about
4.5k sf in the core, 5.2k, 7.4k, and about 9.4k sf at the edge, which is the gradient of a real
town's plats (rowhouse lots downtown, suburban house lots at the fringe). Industrial lots are
unchanged. Fewer, larger lots (1,348 → 1,071 on one map).

## Round 12 — drawdowns

Five 100-year towns (maps 2809232996, 2954813494, 1001, 2002, 3003). The worst real peak-to-trough
fall in value was 85-89% in every class, in every run. The worst US markets on record lost roughly
half (Houston office 1983-87, Manhattan 1990-92); San Francisco 2001-03 lost about 65% of effective
office rent but 30-40% on transactions.

Traced month by month on map 2002 (office) and map 3003 (industrial):

1. **The cap rate priced one rent cycle twice.**
   - `retExp`, which the capital-flows term reads, is trailing RENT growth plus yield. That is the
     same signal as `growthExp`, on a shorter memory. Only `growthExp` answered to the
     supply-price bound.
   - Office caps went 4.3 at the peak to 13.6 at the trough of one cycle, with flows on its ±1.3
     rail for years either side. Industrial went 3.55 to 11.9.
   - Now one trend, bounded once. A falling trend is not extrapolated below replacement cost: no
     new space is coming, and rent must climb back toward the cost of the stock (DiPasquale &
     Wheaton 1996; Sivitanides, Southard, Torto & Wheaton 2001). The years to fill empty floors stay
     in `vacRisk`.
2. **The glut curve is fitted to effective rent, but was applied to asking.** The concession dial
   then cut effective up to another 30%. Asking now carries only what the package has not
   delivered, and never moves against the market. This matches Manhattan four years after its
   shock: asking -3.3%, net effective -17.3%.

### Still open

These are still deeper than a typical market's worst century, which runs about -50 to -65% real
(Depression, early 1990s, GFC).

- **Appraisals carry operating leverage.** Operating expenses do not fall with rent, so a 40% fall
  in rent takes NOI down 60-65%. That is real economics.
- **The excess is in the rent cycle's height.** Traced on the fixed engine, office starts follow
  the first site that pencils within a year, and the first site pencils when rent reaches
  replacement cost. That is textbook. But a 2.3M sf office market is a handful of towers, so the
  wave of starts at the peak delivers 10-30% of the stock at once. The bust that follows is a
  small market's lumpiness (compare Midland, Texas, 1986), and it should shrink as a town grows.
- **Not done:** long-run real rent growth (office ends 1.2-3.1x its opening real rent) is task 27,
  office supply lag.

## Round 13 — land waits its turn; tenants and firms read what they pay; rent meets its supply price

Traced crash by crash on the engine's own appraisals, the remaining value collapses had several causes.

1. **The land residual priced every lot as if it alone got the next building.** At an office peak
   (map 2954813494), 447-467 standing lots had office as their highest use: a 55M sf queue in a town
   with about 5M sf of office, which absorbs about 50k sf a year. Land is now worth the residual at the
   date the lot's turn comes. Sites are taken best first, each waits behind the floor area of better
   sites at the class's measured take-up (net absorption plus stock lost to demolition), and the wait
   is discounted at the asset's through-cycle yield. This is the "financially feasible" test of highest
   and best use (Appraisal Institute) and the real-options view of land (Titman 1985; Capozza &
   Helsley 1990). There is no cap.
2. **Space demand read the asking rent, and the "sector priced out of town" ratchet read the spot
   asking rent.**
   - Since round 12 the glut is priced through concessions, so tenants never saw cheap space.
   - A boom fired the ratchet for seven years on map 2002 and deleted 30% of office demand
     permanently, with office jobs flat. Vacancy then reached 43%.
   - Demand now reads effective rent. Relocation reads the in-place rent: effective rent rolled at
     the class's lease term.
3. **Rent had no pull toward its supply price** (Hendershott 1996; Hendershott, MacGregor & Tse 2002;
   0.20 a year, the middle of 0.15-0.30).
   - It is one-sided. Stock is durable, so rent below replacement cost is not pulled up (Glaeser &
     Gyourko 2005).
   - Pulled both ways, three towns of five shrank to 21-25k.
4. **A 90-floor, 1.5M sf tower was priced on one lot in a 3.2M sf office market**, as filling in 114
   months under a 3x size cap. Lease-up now cannot outrun the class's measured absorption, so the
   operating-deficit reserve sizes schemes to the town. No cap on floors.
5. **The yearly site sample was re-drawn at random**, so its P97 hurdle leapt 1.00 -> 2.03 -> 0.81 and
   switched the cap-rate growth premium on and off. It is now a fixed panel per town.

### Still open, measured

- **Income-only value** (no land at all) still falls 81% for office and 76% for industrial. So the
  remaining depth is how a building's income is valued, not land.
- **Every unowned building is valued as if its whole roll re-signed at spot rent each month.** Owned
  buildings are valued on their leases. In-place lease valuation (round 14, held) takes office to -75%
  and retail to -73%.
- **Round 14 also cuts population about 11%**, because developers size apartment schemes off their
  marked balance sheets. That is under investigation.
- `costOfSpace`, the employer's cost signal, still reads the asking office rent.

## Rounds 15-17 — what tenants pay, what owners believe, what leases are worth, and growth that fades

1. **Firms' cost of space reads the in-place rent.** It used to read the asking quote, which since
   round 12 holds up through gluts.
2. **Land owners ask what they believe.**
   - Owners learn the land market at appraisal-smoothing speed: about 40% of the gap a year
     (Geltner 1993).
   - Each owner carries a lasting 15% log misread of their own lot (the upper end of appraisal error,
     Webb 1994).
   - Developers in direct approaches pay the owner's price, so they find bargains (Garmaise &
     Moskowitz 2004).
   - Public listings price off comps, with the owner's belief as a floor. Overvaluing owners sit
     unsold, which is how a land bust goes quiet.
3. **Every building is valued at market plus its in-place leases.** This is the above/below-market
   lease adjustment of the income approach; owned and unowned buildings are now one quantity.
4. **Cap rates capitalise only growth that persists.** Current growth lowers the cap by
   g0 × y/(y + kappa), with kappa = -ln(0.6) from rent-growth autocorrelation. Before, half of
   trailing growth was capitalised as if permanent, and office caps hit 5.3% on a 5.1% loan index.

### Measured and rejected

peak vacancy 20% -> 31%. It deepened the overbuild cycle.

### Open: the opening state

Office opens at less than half its replacement rent. That is the 2024 secondary-market relationship
(RSMeans $295/sf against $30-35 rents), in which new office does not pencil. With 1-1.5%/yr job
growth, towns then spend 45-58 years in 100 with office vacancy five points under natural, while rent
climbs toward the price at which anything is built. The boom that finally delivers overshoots.
