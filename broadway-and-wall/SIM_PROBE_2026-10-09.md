# Economy probe — 2026-10-09

> **Status: the plan below has been carried out.** See "Executed", "Round 2" and
> "Round 3" at the end for what changed, before/after measurements, what got worse
> and what remains open.

Question asked: where does the economy fail to pull on itself, and where is a
rail or a rule doing the work a market should do?

Engine at `71bd446`. All runs are no-player worlds (resurrected on game over),
50 years, monthly samples. Probes are in `tools/econprobe/`; commands at the end.

| run | what |
|---|---|
| series | 8 cities × 600 months: rents, vacancy, caps, starts, stock, macro |
| supply | 4 cities × 600 months: pencil, friction floor, crews, land via `landPsfNow`, local vs national prices |
| pencil | 2 cities, 5 dates: every vacant lot underwritten at market land and at **free** land |
| rentdec | 2 cities: the monthly rent equation split into its terms, by market state |
| costdec | 2 cities: the construction-cost equation split into its terms |
| X1, X3 | counterfactual builds (see "Rejected hypotheses") |
| `pnpm rails` | 3 towns × 50 years |

## What is healthy

Worth saying first, because the fixes below must not break it.

- **Rent moves the right way.** Real effective rent growth against the vacancy
  gap is negative in every class (slope −0.37 to −2.1, corr −0.39 to −0.70).
  When a class is short, real rents rise 1–6%/yr. When it is soft, they fall
  2–5%/yr.
- **Value leads building** for office, flats and sheds: corr(Tobin's q, starts
  over the next 24 months) is 0.47–0.62.
- The central bank reacts to inflation (corr 0.20–0.73). The real policy rate
  sits at 1.4–2.8%. Determinism and save/load round trips are exact.

## Findings, ranked

### 1. Shortages are rationed by a queue, not by price, and they last for decades

| class | months on the frictional vacancy floor | months with zero starts | zero-start months among floor months |
|---|---|---|---|
| office | 33–55% | 56–100% | 37–99% |
| multifamily | 52–72% | 18–62% | 15–66% |
| industrial | 0–45% | 88–100% | 59–100% |
| retail | 0–31% | 96–100% | 100% where pinned |

Median vacancy over 8 × 50 years is 6.3% for office (natural 11.5%) and 3.5%
for flats (natural 4.5%). Flats sit within 0.3pp of their floor in **66% of all
months**.

**Supply cannot answer.** The `pencil` run underwrote every vacant lot with land
at **zero**. For office, the best lot in the city (P97) earns 6–68% of the
required yield across five dates and two cities. Flats get 21–88%, and sheds get
30–133% with only one date clearing. In seed 1000 at month 120, office is on its
floor and the one vacant lot zoned for office yields 36% of what it needs
even with free land. The
land market is not what blocks it: at market land the hurdles are almost the
same. **The rent level sits far below what it costs to build.**

**Price is not allowed to close that gap fast enough.** When a class is pinned,
the rent equation adds about 3%/yr real (`rentdec`). That comes from
`vacTerm ≤ 0.45%/mo`, `rentPress ∈ [−0.8%, +0.75%]/mo` and a `scarcity` EMA with
a ten-year time constant. Unmet demand meanwhile sits in `structTight` as a
queue of tenants who are not housed and do not bid. In a real market that queue
does not exist. Rent rises until enough of it gives up (`affordEff`) or until
new supply pencils. Here demand is rationed by quantity, and the adjustment
speed is a set of clamps.

The opening state makes this worse. At month 24 every vacant lot fails even
with free land (office P97 0.11–0.23). The opening rent table and the
construction-cost table were never reconciled with each other, so the city
starts decades below replacement cost.

### 2. Construction cost: two rails pulling against each other (hidden from `pnpm rails`)

`costdec`, mean contribution to cost growth:

| seed | inputs (CPI + wages) | `heat` (trades busy/idle) | recession term | **`fairRealCost` pull** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1000 | +3.34%/yr | **−1.91%/yr** | −0.07 | **+0.74%/yr** |
| 16838 | +4.27%/yr | **−0.71%/yr** | −0.05 | **+0.13%/yr** |

- The `heat` term is negative on average for the whole century. Its slope is
  asymmetric (0.0026 below full use, 0.0016 above). `crewIdx` adapts at 1%/mo
  × gap. `CREW_MIN = 0.5` binds in 21% of months in seed 1000. The trades never
  shrink to fit the work, so idle-trade deflation never ends.
- `fairRealCost = 1.004^years` is an **asserted path** that real cost is pulled
  toward. Its floor push is active in **41–89% of all months** (8 seeds). Real
  cost still ends 18–46% below it, and real cost falls 0.0–0.8%/yr while real
  wages rise about 1%/yr on a 45% labour share.
- It is hand-written `Math.min/max`, so `tools/rails.mjs` cannot see it.

The real mechanism: idle trades leave the industry until the ones who stay are
normally busy. Slack cuts real cost for a few years, not for a century. The
fix is crews that adapt fully, a symmetric slope (or one calibrated so its mean
is zero at equilibrium), and deleting `fairRealCost`.

### 3. The town has its own price level, and it is not made of anything local

- Local CPI grows 0.5–0.7pp/yr faster than the nation. By year 50 the town's
  price level is **1.27–1.39× the nation's** (4 seeds).
- The cause is the Phillips term `tight = 0.055 − u_local + vacRate`. Local
  unemployment averages 4.2% while the nation averages 6.5–7.1%, so the term
  is positive almost all the time.
- **Three natural rates of unemployment** sit in the code: 4.2% (national
  expansion target), 4.8% (national Phillips u*), 5.5% (local pivot). That is
  fake number #3: one quantity with three answers.
- Local CPI has **no shelter component**. The town's own rents never enter its
  price level. Shelter is the channel through which real metro price levels
  actually diverge from the nation's.

Proposed: local CPI = national goods (tradables) + local shelter (the engine's
multifamily rent) + local services (local wages). Then the labour-pivot term
goes. Rents feed prices, prices feed wages, and wages feed what tenants can
pay. That is the loop the owner is asking for, and right now it is not
connected.

### 4. The phase label is still a cause in about 60 places

`derivePhase` now dates the cycle from payrolls (good). But the label is then
read as a cause, through step multipliers that jump when job growth crosses a
threshold:

| where | expansion → recession |
|---|---|
| tenant default hazard (`leasing.ts:1475`) | ×0.55 → ×3.4 (**6×**) |
| credit target (`market.ts:1331`) | 1.12 → 0.54 |
| share of listings that are distress (`sim.ts:418`) | 3% → 42% |
| listing volume (`sim.ts:127`) | 1.0% → 0.4% of lots |
| relet downtime, concessions `phaseNudge`, renewal odds, build-cost term, rival behaviour … | |

Most of these already have a continuous driver beside them (`industryStress`,
`sectorMom`, vacancy gap, `creditIdx`), so the label **double-counts**. Credit
in particular: the phase table pulls `creditIdx` at 5.5–16%/mo, and lender
capital (`lenders.ts:867`) pulls at 2.5%/mo. The banks' balance sheets are a
minor input to the credit market.

### 5. Redevelopment is a clock, not a decision

`tickTeardowns` looks at **at most one candidate a month** (an RNG skip, then a
crew cap), and only at buildings at least 45 years old. Over 50 years, 29–43 of
~865 opening buildings were rebuilt (~0.1%/yr; the code's own anchor is 0.5%).
Mean building age went from 65 to 99–107. In a built-out town, redevelopment
*is* the supply curve: a lot is rebuilt when (new value − cost − demolition) >
value as it stands. Throughput should be rationed by crew prices (`heat`), not
by a monthly quota.

### 6. Rents escalate through gluts

With vacancy more than 5pp over natural, nominal effective rent still rose in
**61–76% of office and industrial glut months**. That comes from the
`cheapFloor` CPI carry, which is at its 0.75 ceiling in 75% of calls:
load-bearing. This is the soft-market escalator the file itself calls a fake.
It came back through the "rent is cheap against pay" exception.

### 7. Retail and industrial supply are essentially closed

`sitePencil.retail` = 0 in 96–100% of months in every seed, and industrial in
88–100%. The retail rent-to-wage ratio fell to 0.27–0.98 of its opening.
Retail space per head fell by a quarter. That may be a correct secular story,
but it should come from demand and not from a class that structurally cannot
pencil.

### 8. Cap rates are a formula with no growth term

`capTargetOf` = base + 0.55 × (index − 5.4) + cycle + crunch + vacancy +
flows. Measured slope against the loan index is 0.28–0.37. It has no expected
rent growth (Gordon: cap ≈ r + risk − g). So a class in a decade-long shortage
with +3%/yr real rent growth caps the same as a flat one. Flats carry negative
leverage (cap < loan index) in 33–35% of months.

### 9. Rail count

`pnpm rails`: **58 load-bearing clamps** (45 in August's RAIL_AUDIT), 77
guards, 27 never called. On top of that there are ~200 hand-written
`Math.min/max` pairs and helper clamps (`clampA`, `clampL`, `clamp01`) that are
not instrumented. Finding 2 shows that at least
one of them is load-bearing.

## Rejected hypotheses (measured, then dropped)

- **"The rent-to-income anchor caps rents below replacement."** X1 removed its
  downward pull entirely. Over 4 cities × 50 years, vacancy-on-floor, zero-start
  share, rent growth and q barely moved. One seed was identical to the digit.
  The anchor is not what binds. The adjustment-speed clamps (#1) are.
- **"Pivot local inflation on national unemployment instead of 5.5%."** X3 made
  the drift *worse* (1.30–1.60× by year 50), because the nation's unemployment
  is higher than 5.5% on average. The town is structurally tighter than the
  nation, so any labour-only pivot drifts. That is why #3 proposes a
  component-built CPI.

## Proposed order

1. **Construction cost** (#2). Small, contained, and a hidden rail. Adaptive
   crews, symmetric heat, delete `fairRealCost`. Measure: real cost drift,
   `heat` mean ≈ 0, pull gone.
2. **Local CPI from components** (#3). Connects rents → prices → wages.
3. **Price clears excess demand** (#1). Rent responds to `structTight` through
   the demand elasticity instead of capped flow terms. Re-check the opening
   rent/cost calibration against replacement cost.
4. **Redevelopment as a decision** (#5).
5. **Label → drivers** (#4). Largest change. Each multiplier is replaced by the
   continuous variable it stands for, starting with credit and tenant default.
6. **Glut escalator** (#6) and **cap-rate growth term** (#8).

Each one ships as its own change, with a before/after on these probes,
`pnpm check`, `pnpm gate`, and a baseline attribution.

### What got worse, or is still open

- **Flats still sit on their vacancy floor in ~64% of months**, and redevelopment is still ~0.1%/yr. The market now clears the queue by price (unhoused demand when pinned fell from 2–13% of stock to 0–5%), but this town opens at about 57% of reference rents with costs barely discounted. So building only pencils once rents climb to replacement, and they now do that by themselves. At reference conditions with free land, the P97 site hurdles are MF 1.32, retail 1.42, office 0.90 and **industrial max 0.88**. The industrial rent, cost and cap tables disagree, and that calibration is open.
- **Retail and industrial starts are still rare** (zero in 90–97% of months).
- **The local price level can still end 1.4× the nation's** in the towns where flats are shortest. Its cause is now the right one: rent is a third of the basket, and real pay stays at parity, 0.91–1.04×. But its size inherits the housing-supply calibration above. A metro that cannot build housing does get dear: San Francisco's CPI-U outran the US by about 12% over 1984–2023, so 1.4× over 50 years marks these towns as more supply-starved than any real one.
- **Multifamily negative leverage** went from 35% to 45% of months. The cap now credits real rent growth in a supply-short town. That is historically defensible (MF caps sat at or under mortgage rates for long stretches) but it is a move.
- **Credit crunches are milder** (p5 0.80 vs 0.56): crunch depth now comes from how fast national unemployment rises, and these runs' national recessions are mostly moderate.
- **National labour market**: unemployment averages ~6–7% against a 4.8% u*, which holds national real pay at 0.3–1.0%/yr against 1.1% productivity. Not touched.
- **`pnpm rails`** now reports 62 load-bearing clamps (58 before). The new 0–1 cycle signals are deliberate saturating ramps that the tool counts. Dead anchor code it was counting is deleted. The rails this plan targeted are gone from the list.

## Round 2 — demand (after merging main)

Probe: `tools/econprobe/demand.mjs`. It does two things:
- splits 50 years of each class's demand into its arguments;
- forks a world at year 15 and moves one argument (a class's rent, population, real pay, the cost of money), against a control on the same seed.

All comparisons are against the merged engine on the same seeds.

### How demand is built

`target = opening stock at natural vacancy × driver^ε × affordability(real rent) × income(real pay) × structural level`

| class | driver |
|---|---|
| office | jobs in office trades |
| industrial | jobs × the share of employment that still needs a shed |
| retail | population^0.68 × jobs^0.32 |
| flats | adult households |

Demand then becomes a search (the "pool") and the search becomes occupied space, each step with its own lag.

### What was wrong, measured

1. **The demand curve was flat.** Affordability was clamped to 0.76–1.12, and the cap bound in 42% of office calls. A forked +20% office rent moved occupied office by 0.0% in one year and +0.3% in ten, and the rent was still +12.6% a decade later. With nothing on the demand side answering, the price level was path-dependent. Industrial did not respond at all.
2. **Employers' cost of space was measured against the global rent table, not the town's own opening.** A low-density town read as permanently cheap and drew a standing hiring subsidy.
3. **Rent added job growth and class momentum on top of the vacancy they produce.** That priced demand twice, and asymmetrically: the lift counted only in firm markets, the cut always.
4. **Demand reached occupancy far too slowly, at the same speed for every class.** A year of job growth showed up as 0.17–0.20 of itself in occupied office, and occupied office fell in only 44–53% of job-loss years. A household arriving in town took as long to be housed as a firm takes to fit out a floor. A "matching friction" slowed absorption in gluts, which is the opposite of how search with vacancy works.

### What changed

- The affordability bracket became a guard (0.45–2.2). The cross-section supports elasticity −0.4 over a ~3× rent range.
- Employers' cost of space is now measured against the town's own opening rent-to-pay.
- Rent moves on vacancy and the queue only (the Wheaton–Torto form).
- Demand formation and move-in/move-out run at class-specific lease speeds:
  - forming demand: office 9, retail 6, industrial 6, flats 2 months;
  - moving in: 6 / 4 / 3 / 1.5 months;
  - moving out: 24 / 18 / 18 / 3 months.
- The glut friction is removed, and the 3%/month cap is now a guard.

|---|---|---|
| +20% office rent, rent gap after 3y / 10y | +18.1% / +12.6% (stuck) | +14.9% / −11.3% (unwinds, overshoots) |
| +20% flat rent, occupancy at 1y | 0.0% | −2.0%, half the rise gone by 3y |
| population +10%, flat rent at 1 / 3 / 10y | +2.5 / +0.9 / +1.3% | +3.0 / +6.4 / +4.7% |
| office occupancy per unit of job growth (12m) | 0.17 | 0.35 |
| occupied office falls in a job-loss year | 53% | 65% (retail 76%) |
| office cap response to the loan index | 0.11 | 0.40 |
| median job growth over 50y | 52% | 57% |

### Then: flats' trend per adult, and rents that reprice at their lease speed

- The apartment-demand eras were reweighted so their mean is the Census headship trend: households per adult 0.455 (1960) → 0.498 (2020), +0.15%/yr. The old mean of +0.43%/yr was about 3× the record.
- Shortage-side rent sensitivity now differs by class:
  - flats 3.0× the office slope (apartment rent growth runs ~2.5%/yr per point of occupancy, RealPage/Axiometrics);
  - sheds 2.4× (~2%/yr per point through 2015–22);
  - office and retail unchanged.

| measure | before | after |
|---|---|---|
| flats on vacancy floor | 83% | **44%** |
| industrial on floor | 39% | 32% |
| real flat rent, p50 | +0.62%/yr | +0.81%/yr |
| +20% flat rent shock, occupancy at 1y / rent left at 3y | −2.0% / +9.1% | −4.3% / +2.5% |
| population +10%, flat rent at 1 / 3 / 10y | +3.0 / +6.4 / +4.7% | +4.4 / +7.5 / +4.3% |

### Worse, or still open

- After the lag change alone, **flats sat on their vacancy floor 75% of months and industrial 46%**: demand reached the supply ceiling sooner, and supply did not answer. The class-specific rent sensitivity above brought these down to 44% and 32%.
- **Retail demand has zero income elasticity**, justified in the code by cross-country floor space. The US time series (real retail sales per head up ~1.5%/yr; space per head up until ~2005) argues for some. Left as is, flagged (re-examined in round 3: stays at zero).
- **Industrial tables:** a $100/sf shed, $8.50 rent, a 7% long-run cap and a 17% developer margin are each defensible. Together they describe 2024 costs and rents priced at long-run caps, under which almost nothing gets built. Rents now climb toward the level where a shed pencils, rather than being tuned there.
- **No tenure choice** (added in round 3). Apartment demand does not respond to mortgage rates (+200bp moved occupied flats −2.8% only at 10 years, through other channels). The relevant evidence (renter share against the user cost of owning) is too thin to calibrate honestly yet.
- **Structural eras for flats average +0.4%/yr** of demand per adult. The Census headship trend is closer to +0.15%/yr, so this may double-count household formation; it is left as is pending better evidence.

## Round 3 — why sheds don't get built, and the remaining open items

Probe: `node tools/econprobe/industrial.mjs` (SEEDS, OFF, HZ), which every five
years reports real rent, vacancy, demand state, cap rate and the P97 / max
development hurdle over every industrial-permitted vacant lot, at market and at
free land. National block: `node tools/econprobe/nation.mjs`.

### What was actually wrong with industrial

The earlier note ("industrial never pencils") was overstated. Shed rents do climb
until building pencils: +60–90% real over 15–30 years with vacancy pinned at the
floor. The fault was **what made it pencil**, and what happened next.

- **Cap rates collapsed to the floor.** Buyers capitalised a decade of ~6%/yr real
  rent growth at half weight, which drove industrial caps to the 2.75% floor
  (3 of 3 worlds). That collapse cleared the hurdle while rent was still well
  short of replacement cost at a normal yield.
- **Everyone broke ground at once.** The class order desk underwrote today's
  vacancy and today's concessions, which stay pinned until the first deliveries
  land. Each boom started 15–20% of stock inside five years against a shortage of
  ~4.5% of stock. Vacancy then went to 16–25% for a decade, real rents fell
  20–35%, and the structural-exit ratchet removed up to 10% of tenants for good.
- **The reference tables price sheds below replacement.** At the long-run
  reference (RENT_BASE $8.50, $100/sf hard cost, 7% cap, 17% margin), the best
  free-land shed site reaches 0.70 of its hurdle; offices reach 1.09, flats 1.47.
  Rents therefore have to discover a level ~40% above the table before supply
  answers. That is left to the market (the point of this round), not retuned.

### What changed

- **Buyers pay for growth only up to the supply price** (`capTargetOf`). The
  annual site sampler now publishes `econ.siteHurdle`: the P97 hurdle of the
  vacant sites the city would examine, including sites that fail. The trend's
  discount to the cap is bounded by `base × (1 − e^−room)`, where room is the log
  rent rise that makes those sites clear; it is zero once they clear. A falling
  trend is untouched (DiPasquale & Wheaton ch. 10: rents are bounded above by
  replacement cost where building is possible).
- **The order desk underwrites the market at delivery** (`devPencils`). Vacancy
  becomes stock plus pipeline, less occupancy, less the unhoused queue, less 18
  months of the tenant base's trend growth. Concessions are those that vacancy
  will command, relative to today's, on the same schedule. With nothing in the
  pipeline the rent is unchanged (`test/glut.mjs` holds this).
- **Tenure choice.** Flats' demand driver is multiplied by the renter share:
  +0.8% per point of the loan index over the town's opening rate, rolled over
  three years. Calibration: 1978→1985 mortgage rates +~4.5 points, homeownership
  65.6% → 63.9% (Census HVS), renter households +~5%, ≈1.1%/pt. 2022–24 reads
  lower, so 0.8.

Tenure, measured with the forked +200bp neutral-rate shock (`demand.mjs`,
3 worlds): occupied flats +0.2% at 1 year and ~0 at 3 years (before: 0.0% /
+0.1%). Flats sit on their vacancy floor ~45% of months, so most of the
response shows up in rent: +3.7% at 10 years against +0.4% before (the
10-year column is noisy). The effect is small, as the calibration says it
should be.

Traced worlds (`industrial.mjs`, 3 worlds × 50 years): caps at the start of a shed
boom were 2.75–3.95 before and 3.1–5.1 after. The 16–25% post-boom gluts and the
permanent tenant exits are gone; the one boom in the three worlds added ~14% of
stock and peaked at 11% vacancy.

### Open items, re-examined

- **Retail income elasticity stays at 0.** The US time series that looked like
  an income effect (shopping-centre space per head roughly tripled 1970–2005)
  tracks the 1954–86 accelerated-depreciation regime and has reversed in the
  e-commerce era while incomes kept rising. Reasoning added at `INCOME_ELAST`.
- **National unemployment is not the bug it looked like.** National block alone,
  40 seeds × 100 years: mean 6.35%, p50 5.7%, p90 8.8%, 14.6 recessions a
  century, 17.5% of months in recession. The US 1970–2024 mean is 6.2%. The real
  gap is relative to the 4.8% natural rate: the model averages ~1.5 points over
  it, the US ~0.8 (CBO's NAIRU averaged ~5.4%). The cause is that expansions end
  with unemployment at 5.9% on average, against 4.7% at US cycle peaks, because
  the recession hazard does not depend on how tight labour is. Recession lengths
  drawn from the NBER record (6–16 / 18–43 months) were tried. They cut months in
  recession to 15.5% but did not move the mean (6.33%), and they were reverted.
  The mean wage-gap drag this leaves is −0.19%/yr against productivity. Open.

## Round 4 — markets stuck on the vacancy floor, and supply that never arrived

Probes: `tools/econprobe/pinned.mjs` (in every month a class sits on its
frictional floor, what the order desk sees and why it starts nothing), the
national block (`nation.mjs`), and a 32-world × 30-year before/after on the same
worlds each time.

### Why markets sat on the floor

The floor itself is physics: a suite that turns over sits dark for a while. Two
faults kept markets there.

1. **Orders never became buildings.**
   - The annual site sampler that sets the order budget underwrote only the
     zoning envelope (a tower). The land residual and the start path already
     pick the best of a height ladder. On a pinned flats month the best site's
     tower read 0.93 at free land; the class pro forma for the walk-up read 1.90.
   - The city's start path ranked its 36 sampled lots on land-read surplus,
     builder residual less asking price. The asking price *is* the builder
     residual wherever a builder wins, and above it everywhere else, so the
     score was ≤ 0 on almost every lot and a $0–12/sf tie-break picked the site.
   - Measured over 2 worlds × 30 years: 90% of groundbreak attempts failed the
     desk, at a median hurdle of 0.29–0.39. Flats orders of 2.0–2.3M sf became
     0.2–1.0M sf of groundbreaks; the rest expired in the book.
   - **Fix:** one height ladder (`schemeFloorLadder`) shared by the land
     residual, the sampler and the start path. The start path takes the lot and
     height with the best street hurdle for the class the book is short of. After
     the fix, failed attempts are near misses (median hurdle 0.81–0.91).
2. **The floor was a constant.** The player's suites already re-let faster in a
   tight market (0.7× of an ordinary turn; 2× in a glut). The city's stock used a
   fixed downtime, so its floor was a fixed kink: 5.4% office, 3.5% shops and
   flats, 2.5% sheds. One function (`reletLag`) is now read by both. In a tight
   market the floor falls to ~4% office, ~2.5% shops, ~1.8% sheds and ~3.2% flats.

### National unemployment, closed

Expansions pulled unemployment toward 4.2%, the *average* late-cycle low, and an
exponential approach never reaches its target. So expansions ended at 5.9%
against 4.7% at US cycle peaks. Aimed at the actual lows (3.4–3.8% in 1969, 2000
and 2019), the national block reads:

| | before | now | US 1948–2024 |
|---|---|---|---|
| mean | 6.35% | 5.84% | 5.7% |
| median | 5.7% | 5.3% | 5.5% |
| p10 | 4.4% | 3.8% | 3.9% |
| months under 4.8% | 25% | 38% | ~33% |

An overheating term in the recession hazard was also tried. It double-counts the
policy-rate term, which already raises the hazard when labour is tight, so it was
not adopted.

### Measured and left alone, with reasons

- **Lumpy construction.** In 82–91% of months there are no office or shed
  orders. This town has ~4M sf of office and ~0.6M sf of sheds: a market that
  size sees a new office building every few years, so most months empty is the
  expected print. The unrealistic part was orders that never became buildings,
  which is fixed above. Industrial stock is flat over 30 years in the median
  world because shed demand is flat (secular decline plus exit), not because
  supply is blocked.
- **Office space per job falls** 0.3–0.6%/yr. This comes from the office demand
  eras (round 2 decomposition: −0.14 to −0.31 log points over 50 years), against
  a US record of falling office space per worker since the 1990s. It is a
  modelled trend, not a leak.
- **Sheds open below replacement cost.** At the reference tables a new shed
  needs rent ~40% above the base, so rents climb for years before sheds are
  built. That is the market discovering the level, and how it plays out after
  round 3 is above. Retuning the tables to remove the climb would be the fake
  number.
- **Path dependence.** A single change sends a world down a different path, so
  every comparison here uses the same 32 worlds. At 12 worlds, differences of
  ±50% in glut months were noise.

### Then: frictional vacancy from turnover and matching

The re-let fix still bounded a turn at 0.7 of ordinary, which is a fixed floor
one level down. Frictional vacancy is now what it is in the literature: the
share of tenants leaving each year × how long their space waits.

- **Turnover per class.** Every class had assumed an 8-year lease and counted
  every expiry as a move-out. Now:
  - office: 1/8 a year × 45% not renewing (CBRE and JLL expiry studies);
  - shops: × 35% not renewing;
  - sheds: × 25% not renewing (Prologis retention 70–80%);
  - flats: 47% of households a year (NMHC / RealPage).
- **Flats re-let in about a month** (RealPage days-vacant ~25–40), not the shops'
  3.5 months. The two errors had cancelled: 12.5% × 3.5 months and 47% × 1 month
  both print ~3.8% frictional.
- **Downtime above make-ready is search.** It scales with searchers per vacancy
  at the 0.5 matching elasticity (Petrongolo & Pissarides 2001; Wheaton 1990 for
  office). Searchers are this month's movers plus unhoused demand.
- **Make-ready is the only floor, and it is physical:** flats 0.4 months, office
  2.5 (fit-out), shops 1.5, sheds 1. At an infinite queue the floor reads ~1.6%
  for flats, ~1.2% office, ~0.5% shops and ~0.2% sheds. Record lows are ~2% for
  apartments, 1–3% for primary office in 2000 and 0.6–1% for industrial (2022).
  Rent rations long before then.

| 32 worlds × 30y | round 4 | matching |
|---|---|---|
| months at own minimum: office / shops / flats / sheds | 29 / 12 / 27 / 19% | **18 / 8.5 / 21 / 14%** |
| vacancy p5: office / shops / flats / sheds | 3.9 / 3.7 / 3.1 / 1.8% | 1.4 / 2.8 / 3.3 / 0.3% |
| vacancy p50: office / flats | 9.1 / 4.3% | 9.0 / 4.3% |

| job growth (median) | 22.5% | 22.2% |

Median vacancy, rents and growth are unchanged. Only the tight tail moved. The
office and shed lows are aggressive but within what small markets record; this
town has ~0.6M sf of sheds.

### Still open
